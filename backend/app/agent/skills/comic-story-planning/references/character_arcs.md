# Character Agency, Voice & Arc Mechanics

Believable comic narratives are character-driven. The plot is not what happens to characters; it is what happens because characters act.

---

## 1. Character Agency: Want vs Need

1. **The External Want**: The conscious, tangible goal the character pursues throughout the story (e.g. win the bounty, fix the radio, reach the summit).
2. **The Internal Need**: The emotional, spiritual, or moral truth the character must accept to grow (e.g. learn to trust a partner, accept vulnerability, let go of past guilt).
3. **The Arc**:
   - *Flat Arc (Iconic Hero)*: The protagonist's conviction remains constant, but their steadfastness transforms the world around them (classic Tintin, Sherlock Holmes).
   - *Positive Change Arc*: The character overcomes a lie or blind spot and achieves their internal need, even if it requires altering their original external want.
   - *Tragic Arc*: The character clings to their flaw despite mounting warnings, leading to self-destruction or alienation.

---

## 2. Voice Differentiation & Dialogue Craft

In comics, dialogue is precious real estate. Dialogue balloons occupy artwork space and slow reading tempo. Every word must fight for its right to exist.

### Rules of Comic Dialogue
1. **Word Count Discipline**:
   - **Target**: 5 to 8 words per line.
   - **Hard Cap**: 12 words maximum for newly drafted dialogue.
   - *Why*: A 12-word line fits comfortably in a standard comic balloon without dwarfing the artwork or forcing microscopic typography.
2. **Character Voice Signature**:
   - Contrast characters' rhythm and diction:
     - *Character A (Analytical, cautious)*: Terse, technical terms, complete clauses, rarely uses contractions ("The voltage is unstable.").
     - *Character B (Impulsive, optimistic)*: Fragmented, colloquial, enthusiastic ("Push it! We're close!").
3. **Subtext and the Unsaid**:
   - Characters rarely say what they actually mean when emotions run high.
   - Example (Poor): "I am afraid you don't love me anymore."
   - Example (Subtextual): Character stares at an old ticket stub, pauses, and whispers: "You kept this?"
4. **Silence as Dialogue**:
   - When a character receives shocking, hurtful, or awe-inspiring news, the most powerful response is often a silent beat (speech bubble: `none`). A character's eyes, clenching jaw, or turned shoulder convey tenfold the emotion of a generic "Oh no!"

---

## 3. Relationships and Conflict Geometry

- Never design a cast of characters who all agree on the best course of action.
- Distinct priorities create natural friction without requiring villainy:
  - Pragmatist vs Idealist.
  - Cautious planner vs Bold improviser.
  - Veteran with trauma vs Eager rookie.
